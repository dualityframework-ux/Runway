Case study

How low will my balance get before payday?

Why I built a forward-looking cash-flow planner instead of another budget tracker, how the forecast and payoff math work, and what it still can't do.

Ian KachadorianOctober 20265 min read

The problem

Most budgeting apps answer a question about the past: where did my money go? When you are paid every two weeks and juggling a handful of bills and cards, the question that matters is about the next few weeks:

  • What is the lowest my checking account will get before the next paycheck?
  • How much can I send to a credit card today without cutting it close?
  • If I pay an extra $100 a month, when am I actually debt-free?

I was answering these by hand with a spreadsheet. Runway started as my own tracker, then I generalized it so anyone could use it.

The approach

1. Walk the calendar one day at a time

The forecast starts from today's balance and steps forward 60 days. Each bill lands on its due day, clamped to the last day of shorter months, and is skipped if it is already checked off. Each paycheck lands on its schedule. Everyday spending is spread evenly across every day, so the line drifts down between bills the way a real account does.

balance(day) = balance(day − 1) − monthly spending budget × 12 ÷ 365 − bills due that day + paychecks that day

The lowest point in that series is the number that matters. Subtract the safety floor you set, and what is left is the amount you can safely put toward debt right now.

2. Simulate the debt payoff month by month

Each month, every balance accrues interest at its APR ÷ 12 and gets its minimum payment. The extra payment, plus any minimums freed up by accounts already paid off, goes to one target: the highest APR first (avalanche) or the smallest balance first (snowball). Running the simulation twice, with and without the extra payment, shows exactly what the extra money buys.

3. Make privacy a feature

Runway never asks for a bank login. Everything is typed in and saved in the browser on your own device, with a copy-and-paste backup to move it. That costs automatic syncing, but plenty of people would rather type five numbers than hand over bank credentials.

4. Open on a working example

A first-time visitor sees a full forecast built on clearly labeled sample numbers, not an empty form. One tap clears the sample and goes to setup.

The result

The extra $100 a month moves the debt-free date from January 2033 to December 2030. The app is live and can be added to a phone's home screen with its own icon.

The limits

  • It is only as current as the last balance update. There is no bank feed, so a forecast drifts if the balance is not refreshed on payday.
  • Spending is spread evenly. A big grocery run on one day can push the real low point below the forecast. The safety floor exists to absorb that.
  • Data lives on one device. The backup works, but it is manual.

What I would build next

  1. An optional encrypted sync, so the same plan works on a phone and a laptop without a bank login.

Update: promo-rate end dates are built. A card can carry a promo rate, the month it ends and the APR after. The simulation switches rates on schedule, re-ranks the payoff order when the rate jumps, and warns how much will still be owed when the promo ends. On the sample, a 0% store card would still owe about $580 when its rate jumps to 29.99%.

Update: bills can now repeat every 3, 6 or 12 months, or happen once. They land in the forecast and the monthly checklist only in the months they are due.

Update: the spending log can import a bank statement CSV. It finds the date, description and amount columns, handles banks that split debits and credits, guesses a category from the merchant name, skips deposits and anything already logged, and shows a preview to correct before importing. The file never leaves the device.

Update: it now learns from corrections. Change a merchant's category once and every row from that merchant follows, then future imports use your choice. Learned categories are listed in Setup, where any of them can be removed.

Built with plain JavaScript in one HTML file, no frameworks and no build step. Charts are hand-drawn SVG. State is a single JSON object in browser storage.
